**Promise:** Explain why a plucked string sounds a set of whole-number harmonics, how where you pluck or touch it chooses which ones, and why real strings and most other objects depart from the whole-number pattern.

**Trap:** Every vibrating object has whole-number harmonics; in fact only idealized strings and air columns do, and stiff strings, bars, drums and bells have overtones that are not whole-number multiples.

**Assumes:** waves/standing-waves-and-resonance (fixed ends allow only f_n = n f_1).

## Outline

1. **The ideal string, briefly**: the standing-waves result restated in one line, and the assumptions it needs: perfectly flexible, uniform, fixed ends.
2. **Where you pluck decides the mix**: a pluck at a node of a harmonic cannot excite it, so plucking at the middle removes the even harmonics.
3. **Touching a node**: guitar and violin harmonics, played by lightly touching the string at one half, one third or one quarter of its length.
4. **When the whole numbers fail**: string stiffness raises the upper overtones, and drums and bells have overtone patterns that are not a harmonic series at all.
5. **What this means for you**: what the harmonic series does and does not explain about musical sound.

## Key takeaways

- A string plucked exactly at its midpoint sounds no even-numbered harmonics, in the ideal case.
- Lightly touching a string at one n-th of its length silences every mode without a node there and leaves harmonic n and its multiples.
- Stiffness makes a real string's overtones sharper than whole-number multiples, and piano tuners stretch octaves to follow it. [verify]
- Struck bars, membranes and bells have overtones that are not integer multiples of the lowest one, which is part of why they sound less clearly pitched. [verify]
