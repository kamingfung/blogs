**Promise:** Give a colleague feedback on a draft, a talk or a poster that names what works as precisely as what does not, so they know what to keep as well as what to change.

**Trap:** Starting with strengths is politeness padding, the bread in a feedback sandwich; in fact a specific strength is information, and padding that hides the problem is a different thing.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/formative-assessment (feedback during the work, aimed at the next attempt).

## Outline

1. **Why the sandwich fails**: generic praise around a criticism teaches the receiver to ignore the praise and wait for the "but".
2. **A strength is an instruction to keep**: "the second figure answers the question in one look" tells the author what not to cut in revision; "nice work" tells them nothing.
3. **Specific, about the work, aimed at the next draft**: the same three properties for strengths and for problems, and how to phrase each as an observation, not a verdict.
4. **Peer to peer, without authority**: feedback between equals is optional to act on; ask what the author wants feedback on before giving it.
5. **What this means for you**: a short template for written comments on a colleague's draft.

## Key takeaways

- Feedback that names a specific strength changes what the receiver keeps in the next draft; generic praise does not. [verify]
- Feedback aimed at the task improves performance more reliably than feedback aimed at the person. [verify]
- A meta-analysis found that a substantial share of feedback interventions made performance worse, not better. [verify]
- Asking the author what they want feedback on raises the share of comments they act on. [verify]
