**Promise:** Plan a lecture or a guest session in your own research area that starts from what students can already do, not from what you find interesting this year.

**Trap:** Knowing a topic deeply makes you good at teaching it; in fact expertise hides the steps a novice needs, so experts often skip them without noticing.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/backward-design (start from what students should be able to do).

## Outline

1. **The expert blind spot**: an expert recognizes patterns in one step that a novice has to do in five, and no longer remembers the five.
2. **Research and teaching quality are not the same skill**: what the evidence says about the link between a researcher's output and their teaching ratings.
3. **Teach the field, not your paper**: choose the idea students will need in five years; your result is one example of it, not the syllabus.
4. **Recovering the novice steps**: ask a student to think aloud on a problem, and write down every step you would have skipped.
5. **What this means for you**: a plan for one session in your area, built backward from one outcome.

## Key takeaways

- Experts consistently underestimate how long novices take to do tasks in the expert's field. [verify]
- A large meta-analysis found almost no correlation between research productivity and teaching quality. [verify]
- A session built around one transferable idea, with your own research as the example, is remembered longer than a tour of recent results. [verify]
- A novice's think-aloud on a single problem reveals steps an expert omits from explanations.
