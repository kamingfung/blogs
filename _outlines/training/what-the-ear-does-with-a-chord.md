**Promise:** Explain consonance as what happens when the harmonics of two notes meet inside the ear's frequency filters, and say why the same interval sounds rougher low on a piano than high.

**Trap:** Consonance is a property of the ratio itself, or else purely cultural; in fact it arises from how the ear filters sound, which depends on register and timbre, with learning layered on top.

**Assumes:** training/intervals-as-ratios (interval names and ratios); training/why-a-string-has-harmonics is needed for the harmonic picture.

## Outline

1. **The cochlea as a bank of filters**: the inner ear separates a sound into overlapping frequency bands, each roughly a fixed fraction of an octave wide in the middle range. [verify]
2. **Roughness from shared bands**: two tones in the same band produce roughness; two tones in separate bands do not, whatever their ratio.
3. **From two tones to two notes**: each note brings its harmonics; simple-ratio intervals line their harmonics up or keep them apart, complex ratios put them in each other's bands.
4. **Register, timbre and learning**: why low thirds sound muddy, why pure tones make consonance weaker, and what cross-cultural studies add.
5. **What this means for you**: the same chord voiced low and high, predicted from the filter picture before listening.

## Key takeaways

- Two pure tones sound roughest when they are separated by a fraction of one auditory filter's width, not at any fixed ratio. [verify]
- Because the filters are wider in relative terms at low frequencies, the same interval sounds rougher in a low register than a high one. [verify]
- With pure sine tones, the preference for simple-ratio intervals is much weaker than with harmonic tones. [verify]
- Listeners with little exposure to Western music show weaker preferences for Western consonant intervals, so learning is part of consonance. [verify]
