**Promise:** Explain an instrument as three parts, an exciter, a resonator and a radiator, and say which of the three, and which materials in them, set how it sounds.

**Trap:** A flute and a violin sound different mostly because of what they are made of; in fact how the vibration is driven and the shape of the resonator matter more, and wall material matters little for wind instruments.

**Assumes:** training/why-a-string-has-harmonics (which harmonics a vibrator can produce).

## Outline

1. **Exciter, resonator, radiator**: a bow, reed, lips or hammer starts the vibration; a string or air column selects frequencies; a body or bell couples them to the air.
2. **How the drive shapes the spectrum**: a bowed string, a struck string and a reed each give a different mix of harmonics on the same note, and a different attack.
3. **What the material does**: stiffness, density and internal damping set how fast each harmonic decays and how well a soundboard radiates; why spruce is used for tops and brass for bells.
4. **Where material matters less than believed**: blind listening tests on flutes of different metals and on old versus new violins. [verify]
5. **What this means for you**: an instrument read as a chain from drive to air, with the materials placed in it.

## Key takeaways

- On the same note, the way the vibration is driven changes the harmonic mix more than the material of a wind instrument's tube does. [verify]
- A material's stiffness-to-density ratio and its internal damping are the properties that matter for a soundboard. [verify]
- In blind tests, players and listeners have not reliably preferred old Italian violins over good new ones. [verify]
- The attack and decay of a note carry as much of an instrument's identity as its steady harmonic mix. [verify]
