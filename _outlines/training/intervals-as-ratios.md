**Promise:** Translate the school names of intervals (major third, perfect fifth, minor sixth) into frequency ratios and semitone counts, and derive the major scale from three ratios instead of memorizing it.

**Trap:** Interval names are arbitrary labels to memorize; in fact the number counts letter names, the quality records which of two sizes you have, and both follow from the ratios.

**Assumes:** waves/the-mathematics-of-music (simple ratios, the comma, equal temperament); training/why-the-theory-never-stuck (the plan for this part).

## Outline

1. **Why a fifth is called a fifth**: interval numbers count letter names inclusively, which is why two fourths make a seventh, not an eighth.
2. **Major, minor, perfect**: perfect intervals have one common size and the others come in two; map each name to a semitone count and to its nearest simple ratio.
3. **Inversion is division**: a fifth up and a fourth up make an octave because 3:2 times 4:3 is 2:1; every inversion rule follows.
4. **The major scale from three chords**: three major triads in the ratio 4:5:6 on the tonic, the fifth above and the fifth below give all seven notes.
5. **What this means for you**: a retrieval exercise that rebuilds the interval table from ratios, with no table to copy.

## Key takeaways

- An interval's number counts letter names inclusively, so adding intervals means subtracting one from the sum of their numbers.
- An interval and its inversion multiply to the octave ratio 2:1, so their numbers always sum to nine.
- Three 4:5:6 triads, on the tonic and a fifth above and below it, give every note of the just major scale.
- An interval table rebuilt from ratios is recalled better months later than one memorized as a list. [verify]
