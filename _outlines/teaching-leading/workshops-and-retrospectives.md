**Promise:** Facilitate a workshop or team retrospective that alternates between widening the options and narrowing them, and ends with a short list of changes, each with an owner.

**Trap:** A good retrospective is a good conversation; in fact a retrospective that ends without owned actions changes nothing, and the team learns that saying what went wrong is pointless.

**Assumes:** Nothing in the series file; reads best after teaching-leading/running-a-meeting-like-a-class and teaching-leading/psychological-safety.

## Outline

1. **A workshop is a class where the group writes the content**: the facilitator designs the process and the outcome, not the answer; the role is closer to a lab instructor than a lecturer.
2. **Diverge, then converge**: separate the phase that generates many options from the phase that chooses among them; mixing them lets early critique kill ideas.
3. **The retrospective as formative assessment for the team**: what went well, what did not, what to change; the point is the next cycle, not the verdict on the last one.
4. **From discussion to owned actions**: cap the actions at a few, give each an owner and a date, and open the next retrospective by checking them.
5. **What this means for you**: a one-hour retrospective plan with timed phases, and the habit of reviewing last time's actions first.

## Key takeaways

- Keeping idea generation and idea selection in separate, timed phases produces more options to choose from than mixing them [verify].
- Limiting a retrospective to a small number of actions, each with one owner and a date, makes it likely that each is done.
- Opening each retrospective by reviewing the previous actions shows the team that the meeting changes things.
- A facilitator who also argues for an outcome loses the group's trust in the process; hand facilitation to someone else when you have a stake.
