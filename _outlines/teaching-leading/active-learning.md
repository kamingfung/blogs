**Promise:** Design one active-learning activity from an outcome: a prompt, a task every learner does, and a debrief that checks the result.

**Trap:** Any activity that keeps learners busy is active learning; it counts only when each learner does the thinking the outcome asks for, and the debrief closes the loop.

**Assumes:** Nothing required; teaching-leading/belonging-and-engagement already gives the evidence base and a technique catalog, and this module does not repeat them.

## Outline

1. **Why doing beats hearing**: learners remember what they generate and retrieve, not what they hear; describe the mechanism in plain words before any technique `[verify]`.
2. **Start from the outcome**: the activity should ask for the same verb as the outcome; a discussion cannot practice a calculation.
3. **The three parts**: a clear prompt, a task each person does alone before talking, and a debrief where the teacher learns what the room got right and wrong.
4. **Time and size**: fit the activity into a fixed number of minutes; plan what to cut when it runs long; adjust for large rooms and online rooms.
5. **When learners push back**: active learning can feel less productive than a lecture `[verify]`; say why you use it, once, at the start.
6. **What this means for you**: replace ten minutes of your next talk with one prompt, two minutes of solo work and a short debrief.

## Key takeaways

- An activity works only if every learner does the thinking the outcome names; watching others do it does not count.
- Solo thinking time before pair or group talk lets more people contribute.
- The debrief is where the teacher finds out what was learned; an activity without one is busywork.
- Telling learners why you use the activity reduces their resistance `[verify]`.
