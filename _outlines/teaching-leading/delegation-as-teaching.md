**Promise:** Delegate a piece of work as a sequence of lessons: state the outcome, match the support to the person's current skill on that task, and withdraw the support as they show they can do it.

**Trap:** Delegating means handing a task off and stepping back; in fact handing off a task the person cannot yet do sets them up to fail, and keeping it because "it's faster" means nobody else ever learns it.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/backward-design.

## Outline

1. **Delegation has a learning outcome**: beyond getting the task done, decide what the person should be able to do alone next time; that second outcome changes how you hand it over.
2. **Support matched to skill on this task**: the same person can be expert at one task and new to another; teaching calls this scaffolding, and one management model calls it matching style to readiness [verify].
3. **I do, we do, you do**: the gradual-release sequence from teaching [verify]: show it once, do it together, then let them do it with a check-in; skip steps only when they show they are ready.
4. **Check the work, not the person**: agree in advance what you will review and when, so the check-in reads as a milestone, not as distrust.
5. **What this means for you**: pick one task you keep doing yourself, write the outcome and the three handover steps, and set the date you stop doing it.

## Key takeaways

- Writing down what the person should be able to do alone after the task, not just the task itself, tells you how much support to give.
- The right amount of oversight depends on the person's experience with this particular task, not on their seniority.
- Agreeing on review points before the work starts makes checking feel like a planned step instead of a sign of doubt.
- A task you keep because you do it faster stays a task only you can do.
