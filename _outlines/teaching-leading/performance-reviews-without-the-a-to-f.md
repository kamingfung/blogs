**Promise:** Write a performance review that says which stated expectations a person met, with evidence for each, instead of one overall score nobody can act on.

**Trap:** A single rating summarizes a year of work; in fact it averages unrelated skills the same way a letter grade does, and the person reads the number and skips the feedback.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/grading-and-what-happens-when-you-teach, teaching-leading/rubrics.

## Outline

1. **The review is a grade in disguise**: the overall rating repeats every flaw of the A to F scale from the grading module: averaged skills, poor agreement between raters, feedback ignored once a number is attached.
2. **Grade against expectations, not against the curve**: borrow specifications grading; write the expectations for a role as observable outcomes at the start of the cycle, then mark each met or not met.
3. **Evidence, not impressions**: each judgment cites specific work from the whole cycle, not the last month; keep a running log during the year so the review is a summary, not an act of memory.
4. **No surprises**: anything in the review that the person hears for the first time is a failure of the feedback during the year, not news to deliver now.
5. **What this means for you**: a short procedure for your next review cycle: expectations up front, a log, one paragraph of evidence per expectation, and a forward-looking plan kept apart from the rating.

## Key takeaways

- Writing role expectations as observable outcomes before the cycle starts makes the end-of-cycle review shorter to write and easier to defend.
- A review built from a running log of specific work covers the whole period; one written from memory over-weights the last few weeks [verify].
- Separate the development conversation from the rating conversation, because people who hear a number first tend to stop listening to the feedback [verify].
- If a review contains a criticism the person has never heard before, fix the feedback cadence during the year, not the review form.
