**Promise:** Explain why recalling material at spaced intervals beats rereading it, and schedule practice for yourself, a class or a team so that what is learned is still there weeks later.

**Trap:** Studying something many times in one session is the efficient way to learn it; in fact massed review feels fluent but fades fast, while spaced recall feels harder and lasts longer [verify].

**Assumes:** Nothing.

## Outline

1. **Forgetting is the default**: memory for new material drops quickly without review, in a curve first described in the nineteenth century [verify]; the question is when to review, not whether.
2. **Spacing**: the same total practice spread over days produces longer retention than the same practice in one block [verify].
3. **Retrieval beats rereading**: trying to recall material strengthens it more than looking at it again; this is why a low-stakes quiz teaches as well as tests [verify].
4. **Why the better method feels worse**: effortful recall and spacing feel slower in the moment; learners and teachers judge by fluency and choose the weaker method [verify].
5. **What this means for you**: a review schedule for a course or for team onboarding: short recall prompts a day, a week and a month after first contact.

## Key takeaways

- Spreading practice over several sessions leads to longer retention than the same amount of practice in one session [verify].
- Recalling material from memory, even with errors, strengthens later recall more than rereading the same material [verify].
- How fluent a learner feels during practice is a poor guide to how much they will remember later [verify].
- Revisiting a key idea briefly a week after teaching it costs little time and catches what was forgotten.
