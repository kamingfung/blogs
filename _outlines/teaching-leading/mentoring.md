**Promise:** Run a mentoring relationship that the mentee steers: they bring the questions and goals, you ask before you advise, and you separate mentoring from managing and from sponsoring.

**Trap:** Mentoring means telling a junior person how you did it; in fact your own path is one data point from a different time, and advice given before you understand the question often answers the wrong one.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/one-on-ones.

## Outline

1. **Three roles that get confused**: a manager directs the work, a mentor helps the person think, a sponsor spends their own reputation to open doors [verify]; one person can hold several, but say which one you are using.
2. **The mentee owns the agenda**: like a student-centered course, the relationship starts from the learner's goals; ask the mentee to bring a question each time.
3. **Ask before you tell**: questions that help the mentee see their own options first; advice comes after, labeled as one view.
4. **Mentoring outside your reporting line**: why a mentor who does not rate you can hear things a manager cannot, and how to keep that trust.
5. **What this means for you**: a first-meeting script, a rule of thumb for when to ask and when to advise, and a check every few months on whether the mentee's goals have changed.

## Key takeaways

- Saying which role you are in, manager, mentor or sponsor, before giving advice tells the other person how much weight to put on it.
- A mentee who sets the agenda for each meeting gets more from it than one who waits for the mentor to choose topics [verify].
- Asking two questions before giving any advice often shows that the mentee's real question is different from the first one they asked.
- People from groups underrepresented in a field often get mentoring but less sponsorship [verify]; check which one you are actually giving.
