**Promise:** Decide how to publish course material openly: who it is for, which license lets others reuse it, what format survives, and who will keep it current.

**Trap:** Open courseware means putting your files online; in fact files without a license, a stated audience and a maintenance plan are visible but hard to reuse, and go stale quickly.

**Assumes:** Nothing in the series file; reads best after teaching-leading/turning-notes-into-a-course.

## Outline

1. **Why publish teaching material openly**: reach beyond your own students, reuse by other teachers, and a public record that forces clear writing; large university open-course projects showed this at scale [verify].
2. **A license is what makes it reusable**: without one, others cannot legally adapt your material; open licenses differ in whether they allow commercial use and require sharing alike [verify].
3. **Formats that last and can be edited**: plain text sources and a build step are easier for others to adapt than slide files or PDFs.
4. **Open material needs an owner**: a dated version, a way to report errors, and a stated plan for updates; otherwise readers cannot tell what is current.
5. **What this means for you**: a pre-publication checklist: audience line, license, editable source, version date, and a contact for corrections.

## Key takeaways

- Material published without a license cannot legally be adapted by others in most countries, however openly it is posted [verify].
- Choosing a license that requires attribution only allows the widest reuse; adding conditions narrows who can use it [verify].
- Keeping the source in plain text with a build step makes corrections and translations by others practical.
- Putting a version date and a way to report errors on each page tells readers how far to trust it.
