**Promise:** Write a one- to two-page decision memo that states the decision needed, the recommendation, the options weighed and the risks, so a reader can act on it without a meeting.

**Trap:** A memo is a record of the work you did; in fact it is a tool for one reader to make one decision, and the work belongs in an appendix.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/answer-first.

## Outline

1. **Who reads it, and what do they decide**: name the reader and the decision before writing; a memo with no decision is a report, and should say so.
2. **A fixed shape**: decision needed, recommendation, why, options considered, risks and what would change the recommendation, next steps; the reader learns where to look.
3. **Options honestly stated**: present the strongest version of each rejected option; a memo that only argues for one choice reads as a sales pitch and invites a meeting to find out what was left out.
4. **The appendix is where the work goes**: method, data and caveats sit behind the memo for the reader who wants to check, not in front of the reader who wants to decide.
5. **What this means for you**: a template with the six headings, and a self-test: can someone act on the memo without asking you a question.

## Key takeaways

- Naming the decision and the decision-maker in the first two lines tells the reader whether to read on and tells you what to cut.
- A memo that states what evidence would change the recommendation is easier to approve, because the reader can see the writer tested it.
- Moving method and caveats into an appendix shortens the memo without hiding anything from a reader who wants to check.
- Some organizations replace slide decks with written memos read at the start of a meeting [verify]; try it once and compare the questions you get.
