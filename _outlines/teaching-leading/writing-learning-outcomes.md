**Promise:** Rewrite a vague course goal as an outcome that names who will do what, at what level, so that someone else could check it.

**Trap:** A list of topics ("students will cover radiative transfer") reads like an outcome; it is not, because nothing in it can be observed or checked.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/backward-design.

## Outline

1. **From topic to verb**: a topic says what the teacher covers; an outcome says what the learner can do at the end. Show the same goal written both ways.
2. **Why "understand" fails**: words like understand, know and appreciate hide the level of performance; replace each with an action a person could watch or read.
3. **Levels of thinking**: describe the idea that recall, application, analysis and design are different targets, then name it as Bloom's revised taxonomy `[verify]`; a verb list is a prompt, not a rule.
4. **The parts of a usable outcome**: an action, the conditions it happens under, and the standard that counts as good enough; when to keep all three and when one is enough.
5. **What this means for you**: draft three outcomes for one session, read each aloud, and ask "how would I know?"; if the answer is unclear, the outcome is still a topic.

## Key takeaways

- An outcome that a colleague cannot turn into a check is a topic, not an outcome.
- Replace "understand" with the action you would accept as evidence of understanding.
- Pick the verb for the level of thinking you want; recall and design need different tasks `[verify]`.
- Three to five outcomes per course unit are easier to teach to and to assess than a long list `[verify]`.
