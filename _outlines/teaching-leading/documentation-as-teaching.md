**Promise:** Plan documentation by who reads it and what they already know: a tutorial for a newcomer, a how-to for someone with a task, a reference for someone looking up a fact, an explanation for someone who wants to know why.

**Trap:** Good documentation is complete documentation; in fact a page that is complete for every reader teaches none of them, because a newcomer and an expert need different pages.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/meeting-students-where-they-are.

## Outline

1. **A doc is a lesson with no teacher present**: the reader cannot ask a question, so the writer must predict what they know and where they will get stuck.
2. **Four readers, four kinds of page**: a learner, a person with a task, a person checking a fact, a person seeking understanding; one framework names these tutorial, how-to, reference and explanation [verify].
3. **Prior knowledge decides the entry point**: state what the page assumes at the top, and link to the page that teaches it; this is the prerequisite map from course design.
4. **Test it like a lesson**: watch a new reader follow the doc without help; every place they stop is feedback on the doc, not on the reader.
5. **What this means for you**: sort your existing docs into the four kinds, find the page that tries to be all four, and split it.

## Key takeaways

- A page that states at the top what it assumes the reader knows lets the wrong reader leave early and the right reader trust the rest.
- Mixing step-by-step instructions with background explanation on one page makes both harder to follow; split them and link between them [verify].
- Watching one new person use a doc without help finds more problems than a review by the person who wrote it.
- When a question about a doc comes up twice, the answer belongs in the doc, not in the reply.
