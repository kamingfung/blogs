**Promise:** Before work starts, write down the evidence that will show the work is finished, and get the person who asked to agree with it.

**Trap:** Everyone on a project already agrees what "done" means; usually each person holds a different picture, and the gap shows only at delivery.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/backward-design.

## Outline

1. **Backward design, moved to work**: the outcome becomes the result the requester needs; the assessment becomes the evidence that would convince them; the content becomes the plan.
2. **Three pictures of done**: the requester's, the doer's and the reviewer's; show how a request like "clean up the data" means three different end states.
3. **Evidence, not activity**: "done" names something a person can look at (a number, a decision, a working tool), not hours spent or steps taken.
4. **The cheapest moment to disagree**: a short conversation before the work saves rework after it; how to ask "what would you do with this when it arrives?"
5. **What this means for you**: for the next request you receive, write one sentence of done and one of not-done, and send it back before you start.

## Key takeaways

- A definition of done names evidence a requester can inspect, not effort spent.
- Asking "what will you do with this?" exposes the real outcome behind a request.
- Writing down what is out of scope prevents more rework than adding detail to what is in scope.
- Agreement on done costs minutes at the start and saves days at the end `[verify]`.
