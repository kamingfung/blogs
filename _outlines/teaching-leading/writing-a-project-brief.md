**Promise:** Write a one-page brief that lets a capable person start work without a meeting: why, what done looks like, what is out of scope, and what they decide alone.

**Trap:** A good brief is a detailed task list; a task list removes judgment, and the person follows the steps even when the steps stop making sense.

**Assumes:** teaching-leading/decide-what-done-looks-like-first.

## Outline

1. **What a brief is for**: it transfers intent, not instructions, so the reader can make the right call when the plan breaks; describe the idea, then name it as commander's intent `[verify]`.
2. **The sections that earn their place**: the problem and who has it, the outcome and evidence of done, non-goals, constraints (time, data, budget), decisions the reader owns, and who to ask.
3. **What to leave out**: method, unless a constraint forces it; history the reader does not need; every risk you can imagine.
4. **The read-back test**: ask the reader to say back the goal and one thing they would not do; a wrong read-back is a fault in the brief.
5. **What this means for you**: rewrite one recent request as a one-page brief, cut every step that is a method, and run the read-back with the person who gets it.

## Key takeaways

- A brief states intent and the evidence of done, and leaves the method to the reader.
- Listing non-goals and the decisions the reader owns prevents more back-and-forth than adding detail.
- If a capable reader cannot say back the goal in one sentence, rewrite the brief, not the reader.
- One page is a useful limit; a longer brief usually hides an undecided goal.
