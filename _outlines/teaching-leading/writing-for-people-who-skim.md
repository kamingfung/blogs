**Promise:** Lay out any document so a reader who reads only the headings, first sentences and bold labels still gets the argument.

**Trap:** Writing for skimmers means dumbing the text down; in fact skimming is how most readers decide whether to read closely, and a skimmable layout serves the close reader too.

**Assumes:** Nothing.

## Outline

1. **Most readers skim first**: on screens, readers scan headings and the start of lines before deciding to read [verify]; design for that pass instead of fighting it.
2. **Headings that say something**: a heading that states the section's claim gives the skimmer the argument; a heading that names a topic gives them a table of contents.
3. **First sentence carries the paragraph**: put the paragraph's point first, details after; a skimmer who reads only first sentences then reads a summary.
4. **Lists, tables and bold, used sparingly**: a list for parallel items, a table for comparisons, bold only as a run-in label; everything emphasized means nothing is.
5. **What this means for you**: the skim test: read only headings and first sentences of your draft; rewrite every one that does not advance the argument.

## Key takeaways

- If a reader who reads only the headings and first sentences cannot state your argument, the layout is hiding it.
- A heading written as a claim tells a skimmer whether the section is worth reading; a heading written as a topic does not.
- A comparison of three or more items across two or more attributes is faster to read as a table than as prose [verify].
- Bolding many phrases in a paragraph slows the skimmer down, because nothing stands out.
