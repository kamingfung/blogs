**Promise:** Name the waves an earthquake sends out, use the gap between their arrivals to find the distance to the quake, and explain how they revealed a liquid outer core.

**Trap:** Readers expect the first shaking to be the strongest; the fast compressional wave arrives first but is usually weaker, and slower surface waves often bring the strongest shaking.

**Assumes:** waves/transverse-and-longitudinal (the series file lists none).

## Outline

1. **Two body waves**: the P wave pushes and pulls along its path, the S wave shakes across it; P is always faster.
2. **Distance from a time gap**: the delay between P and S arrivals grows with distance, so one station gives a distance and three give a location.
3. **Surface waves**: waves trapped near the surface spread out less, travel slowest, and often do the most damage.
4. **Seeing inside the Earth**: S waves cannot cross a liquid, so a shadow zone on the far side of the planet shows the outer core is liquid; bending of P waves shows the inner core.
5. **Early warning and magnitude**: detecting the P wave gives seconds of warning before stronger shaking; magnitude is logarithmic, so each step is a large jump in energy.
6. **What this means for you**: for anyone working on the Pacific coast of South America, earthquakes are a real hazard, and early-warning alerts rely on the P wave outrunning the damaging waves.

## Key takeaways

- P waves are longitudinal and S waves are transverse, and P waves travel faster in any solid.
- The S minus P arrival time grows with distance from the source, so one station can estimate distance.
- S waves do not travel through liquid, and their shadow zone was key evidence that Earth's outer core is liquid. [verify]
- Each whole step in earthquake magnitude corresponds to roughly 32 times more radiated energy. [verify]
- Earthquake early-warning systems give warning times from a few seconds to tens of seconds, depending on distance. [verify]
