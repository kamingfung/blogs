**Promise:** Explain how an ultrasound scanner turns echo timing into a picture and blood flow into a color, and why it trades depth for detail.

**Trap:** Readers think an ultrasound image shows tissue density like an X-ray; it shows where sound reflects at boundaries between materials, at depths computed by assuming one speed of sound.

**Assumes:** waves/transverse-and-longitudinal and waves/describing-a-wave (the series file lists none).

## Outline

1. **Pulse and listen**: send a short pulse, time each echo, and convert time to depth with an assumed sound speed.
2. **What makes an echo**: reflection happens where acoustic impedance changes; this is why gel is needed and why bone and gas block the view.
3. **Frequency trades detail for depth**: higher frequency gives finer detail but is absorbed faster, so deep organs need lower frequencies.
4. **Building the picture**: an array of elements steers and focuses the beam line by line; speckle is interference, not tissue texture.
5. **Doppler**: echoes from moving blood return at a shifted frequency, and the shift gives speed along the beam.
6. **What this means for you**: ultrasonic level and distance sensors use the same pulse-echo timing, and their reading shifts when air temperature changes the speed of sound.

## Key takeaways

- Scanners convert echo time to depth assuming a soft-tissue sound speed of about 1540 m/s. [verify]
- Echo strength depends on the acoustic impedance mismatch at a boundary, which is why air and bone reflect almost all the sound.
- Attenuation in tissue rises with frequency, so higher-frequency probes give finer resolution and shallower imaging depth. [verify]
- Speckle in ultrasound images is an interference pattern and does not map tissue microstructure directly.
- The speed of sound in air rises with temperature, roughly 0.6 m/s per degree Celsius. [verify]
