**Promise:** Explain why a note and the note at twice its frequency get the same name, and say which part of that is physics and which part is the listener.

**Trap:** Readers assume octave equivalence follows from the 2:1 ratio alone; the ratio explains why octaves blend, but hearing them as "the same note" is a perceptual and partly cultural judgment.

**Assumes:** waves/the-mathematics-of-music and waves/standing-waves-and-resonance (the harmonic series).

## Outline

1. **The puzzle**: men and women sing "the same" tune an octave apart and nobody notices; no other interval behaves like this.
2. **The physics half**: every harmonic of the upper note is also a harmonic of the lower note, so the two notes share their spectrum and fuse with no beating.
3. **The listener half**: pitch has two dimensions, height (higher or lower) and chroma (which note); draw them as a helix where each turn is one octave.
4. **How strong is the evidence**: what tests on listeners from different musical cultures, on infants and on animals suggest, and where the results disagree.
5. **The stretched octave**: listeners often prefer octaves slightly wider than 2:1, and pianos are tuned that way, so the perceptual octave is not the exact ratio.
6. **What this means for you**: chroma is a quantity that wraps around, like hour of day or day of year, so it belongs on a circle, not a line; the same rule applies to any cyclic feature in a model.

## Key takeaways

- The harmonics of a note at 2f are a subset of the harmonics of a note at f, so the pair produces no new beating.
- Pitch perception has a height dimension and a chroma dimension, and octave equivalence is a statement about chroma only.
- Evidence that octave equivalence is universal across cultures is mixed. [verify]
- Listeners tend to prefer melodic octaves slightly wider than an exact 2:1 ratio. [verify]
- Piano tuners stretch octaves partly because real piano strings have harmonics slightly sharper than whole multiples. [verify]
