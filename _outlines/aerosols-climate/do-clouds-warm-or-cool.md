**Promise:** Explain why a cloud both cools the planet (by reflecting sunlight) and warms it (by trapping outgoing heat), and predict which effect wins for low, thick clouds versus high, thin ones.

**Trap:** Clouds either cool (a cloudy day is cold) or warm (a cloudy night is mild); both experiences are true, and the net effect depends on the cloud's height, thickness and the time of day and year.

**Assumes:** basic idea of sunlight in and heat radiated out; no module in this site covers Earth's energy budget yet.

## Outline

1. **Two budgets, one cloud**: sunlight comes in at short wavelengths, the Earth radiates heat out at long wavelengths; a cloud intercepts both.
2. **The shortwave side**: a cloud reflects sunlight that the darker surface below would have absorbed; this works only in daylight and depends on how bright the cloud is.
3. **The longwave side**: a cloud absorbs heat from below and radiates its own heat at its top temperature; a cold, high top sends less heat to space, so high clouds trap more.
4. **Height decides**: low stratus and stratocumulus cool, thin high cirrus warms, deep storm clouds roughly cancel [verify]; the global net is cooling [verify].
5. **What this means for you**: cloud cover on a frost night cuts the night's heat loss, while a cloudy day cuts the sunlight reaching the crop; temperature forecasts that miss cloud cover miss both day highs and night lows.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- Every cloud both reflects sunlight and traps outgoing heat; the net effect depends mostly on its top temperature and its optical thickness.
- Low, thick clouds cool the surface and the planet, while thin, high clouds warm it.
- Averaged over the globe and the year, clouds have a net cooling effect of roughly 20 watts per square meter [verify].
- At night a cloud has no sunlight to reflect, so it can only warm the surface, which is why cloudy nights rarely bring radiation frost.
