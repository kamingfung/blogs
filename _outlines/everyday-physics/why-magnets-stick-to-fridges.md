**Promise:** Explain why a magnet pulls on a steel fridge door but not on aluminum, copper or many stainless steels, from electron spins lining up in small regions.

**Trap:** "Magnets stick to metal." Most metals are barely magnetic; a magnet sticks only to the few materials, such as iron, nickel and cobalt and steels built from them, whose electron spins can line up together.

**Assumes:** Electrons in atoms; a magnet has a north and a south pole. No module required.

## Outline

1. **Not all metals**: try aluminum foil, a copper coin and a stainless pan; only some respond, so "metal" is not the property.
2. **Every electron is a tiny magnet**: in most atoms the electrons pair off and cancel; in iron some do not, and neighboring atoms prefer to point the same way.
3. **Domains**: an unmagnetized iron door is made of many small regions, each fully magnetized but pointing in different directions, so they cancel.
4. **The magnet recruits the door**: near a magnet, domains that point along its field grow, the door becomes a magnet itself, and the two attract; many stainless steels have a crystal structure that does not do this [verify].
5. **What this means for you**: food and grain lines use magnets to catch stray bits of iron and steel, which is why foreign bodies of non-magnetic stainless steel need metal detectors instead [verify].
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- Only a few materials, such as iron, nickel, cobalt and many of their alloys, are strongly attracted to a magnet.
- A magnet sticks to a steel door by temporarily magnetizing the door.
- Common kitchen stainless steels such as type 304 are barely magnetic [verify].
- Flexible fridge magnets have stripes of alternating poles, so they grip on one side only [verify].
