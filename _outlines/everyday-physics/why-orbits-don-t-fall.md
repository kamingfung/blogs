**Promise:** Explain an orbit as an object that is always falling but moves sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it.

**Trap:** "Astronauts float because there is no gravity in space." Gravity at space-station height is still about 90% of its surface value [verify]; astronauts float because they and their station fall together.

**Assumes:** School physics: force, speed, and that gravity weakens with distance. No module required; [Gravitational wells](gravitational-wells) is optional background.

## Outline

1. **Newton's cannonball**: fire a ball sideways from a mountain, faster each time, until its fall follows the curve of the Earth; that is an orbit.
2. **How fast is sideways enough**: low orbit needs about 7.8 km/s [verify], and the speed drops the higher the orbit, so outer satellites lap the Earth more slowly.
3. **Floating is falling**: weight is the push of the floor, not the pull of gravity; in free fall nothing pushes, so everything floats together, the same as in a falling elevator.
4. **Why some orbits do fall**: with nothing to slow it, an orbit keeps its energy; low satellites brush the thin upper air, lose speed and spiral down.
5. **What this means for you**: weather satellites use two kinds of orbit: geostationary ones hover over one spot by matching the Earth's spin, and polar ones pass lower and see the whole globe in strips.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- A satellite in orbit falls toward the Earth all the time; it misses because of its sideways speed.
- Gravity at the height of the International Space Station is about 90% of its value at the surface [verify].
- An object in free fall feels weightless whatever the strength of gravity around it.
- A geostationary orbit sits about 35,800 km above the equator and takes one sidereal day per lap [verify].
- Air drag, not a lack of speed, brings low satellites down, within years at a few hundred kilometers [verify].
