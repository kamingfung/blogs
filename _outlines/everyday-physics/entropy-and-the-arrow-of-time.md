**Promise:** Explain entropy as a count of the microscopic arrangements that look the same from outside, why it rises, and why that rise gives time a direction when the laws of motion do not.

**Trap:** "Entropy is disorder, and it always increases everywhere, so things like ice crystals or living cells break the rule." Entropy counts arrangements, not mess; it can fall in one place if the surroundings gain more, as freezing water does by releasing heat.

**Assumes:** everyday-physics/why-ice-floats (the ordered lattice of ice), as listed in the series file. everyday-physics/how-a-fridge-works helps for heat flowing downhill.

## Outline

1. **The laws run both ways**: a film of two molecules colliding looks fine played backward; a film of an egg breaking does not, so the direction must come from somewhere other than the laws of motion.
2. **Counting arrangements**: entropy measures how many microscopic arrangements match what we see; mixed and spread-out states have vastly more arrangements, so systems drift toward them.
3. **Order can grow if the surroundings pay**: water freezing into ice loses entropy but releases heat that raises the surroundings' entropy by more, as long as it is cold enough.
4. **The arrow comes from the start**: entropy rises toward the future because the universe began in a very low-entropy state [verify]; memories and records point the same way, which ties to the cost of erasing them in everyday-physics/landauer-s-limit.
5. **What this means for you**: frost on a clear night is ordered ice grown from scattered vapor, paid for by heat the ground and leaves radiate to space.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- The microscopic laws of motion work the same forward and backward in time.
- Entropy measures the number of microscopic arrangements consistent with what we observe, not how messy something looks.
- Total entropy of a system and its surroundings does not decrease, but a part can lose entropy if the rest gains more.
- The direction of time we experience traces back to the low entropy of the early universe [verify].
