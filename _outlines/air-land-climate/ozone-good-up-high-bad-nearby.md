**Promise:** Explain why the same molecule shields life in the stratosphere and harms lungs and crops at the ground, and why the two problems need different fixes.

**Trap:** Ozone is ozone, so smog near the ground should help refill the ozone hole; in fact the two layers are made by different chemistry, sit kilometers apart and barely exchange.

**Assumes:** School chemistry. No series modules needed.

## Outline

1. **One molecule, two places**: most ozone sits in the stratosphere and only a small share sits near the ground, where we breathe it. [verify]
2. **How the high layer forms and shields**: hard ultraviolet light splits oxygen molecules, the atoms join other oxygen molecules, and the ozone made absorbs the ultraviolet that would damage DNA and leaves.
3. **How the low layer forms and harms**: near the ground there is too little hard ultraviolet, so ozone comes from sunlight acting on nitrogen oxides and organic gases; as a strong oxidant it inflames airways and enters leaves.
4. **Two problems, two treaties**: chlorine and bromine compounds thin the high layer; combustion and organic gases raise the low layer; one is controlled by the Montreal Protocol, the other by national air quality laws.
5. **What this means for you**: an ozone hole headline and a ground-level ozone alert are about different air; for crops and field workers the ground-level number is the one that matters, and it peaks on hot, sunny afternoons.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- About 90% of the atmosphere's ozone is in the stratosphere and about 10% in the troposphere. [verify]
- Stratospheric ozone forms when ultraviolet light below about 240 nanometers splits oxygen molecules, a source almost absent near the ground. [verify]
- Ground-level ozone forms from nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in sunlight, so it tracks pollution and weather, not the ozone hole.
- Ground-level ozone does not rise far enough or last long enough to repair stratospheric ozone loss.
