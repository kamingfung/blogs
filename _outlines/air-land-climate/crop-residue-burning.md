**Promise:** Explain why farmers burn crop residue, how much it adds to regional air pollution, and why bans alone rarely stop it.

**Trap:** Residue burning persists because farmers do not know better, so a ban fixes it; burning is often the cheapest way to clear a field in a short window between crops.

**Assumes:** air-land-climate/wildfire-smoke and air-land-climate/black-carbon.

## Outline

1. **Why burn**: after a harvest, a short gap before the next sowing leaves little time and money to remove straw; fire clears it in a day.
2. **A case: northwest India**: rice-straw burning in autumn coincides with low winds and cool nights, and smoke adds to winter pollution in the Delhi region. [verify]
3. **Hard to count**: fires are small and brief, so satellites miss many, and emission estimates for a season vary widely. [verify]
4. **Alternatives and why uptake is slow**: machines that sow through straw, baling for fuel, composting and biochar each need equipment, markets or time; bans without support shift burning to night or cloud cover.
5. **What this means for you**: where pruning wood or residues are burned on a farm, the smoke affects workers and nearby communities first; chipping or composting avoids that, at a cost to weigh.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- Small agricultural fires are undercounted by satellite active-fire products, so inventories based on them underestimate emissions. [verify]
- Stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana contributes a varying share of Delhi's PM2.5 in late October and November. [verify]
- Residue burning emits more organic carbon than black carbon, so its net climate effect differs from diesel soot. [verify]
- Enforcement-only bans tend to shift the timing of fires more than they reduce burned area. [verify]
