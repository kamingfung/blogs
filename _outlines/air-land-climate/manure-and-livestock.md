**Promise:** Follow a kilogram of nitrogen from feed to field through housing, storage and spreading, and explain why nitrogen saved at one stage can be lost at the next.

**Trap:** Covering the slurry tank solves manure ammonia; nitrogen kept at one stage stays in the manure and is lost at the next unless every stage is managed.

**Assumes:** air-land-climate/where-ammonia-comes-from.

## Outline

1. **Most feed nitrogen comes out the other end**: animals retain only a small share of the nitrogen they eat, and the rest leaves in urine and dung. [verify]
2. **The manure chain**: housing, storage, treatment, spreading and grazing each lose ammonia, and storage also makes methane and nitrous oxide.
3. **Saving at one stage, losing at the next**: a covered store keeps ammonium in the slurry, which then volatilizes at spreading unless the slurry is injected or worked into the soil; this is often called pollution swapping.
4. **What works at each stage**: lower-protein feed, frequent removal from barns, covers and acidification in storage, injection or band spreading in the field.
5. **What this means for you**: if a farm buys manure or compost as fertilizer, the application method decides much of the ammonia loss; the nitrogen it contains is less predictable than a bag of synthetic fertilizer.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- Livestock retain roughly 10–30% of feed nitrogen and excrete the rest. [verify]
- Livestock manure is the largest single source of global ammonia emissions. [verify]
- Reducing ammonia loss in housing or storage only lowers total emissions if the nitrogen saved is also protected at spreading.
- Injecting slurry into soil can cut spreading ammonia losses by more than half compared with surface broadcasting. [verify]
