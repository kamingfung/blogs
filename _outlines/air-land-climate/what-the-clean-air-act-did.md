**Promise:** Describe the main tools of the US Clean Air Act, what they changed in measured air quality, and what they left out.

**Trap:** US air got cleaner because industry moved abroad; emissions of the regulated pollutants fell steeply at home while the economy grew, and the law's tools account for much of that.

**Assumes:** air-land-climate/units-standards-and-what-unhealthy-means.

## Outline

1. **The structure**: national ambient standards set by health evidence, state plans to meet them, and technology rules for new sources and vehicles. [verify]
2. **Market tools**: the 1990 amendments capped sulfur dioxide from power plants and let them trade allowances, and emissions fell faster and cheaper than forecast. [verify]
3. **What changed**: lead in air nearly vanished after leaded gasoline was phased out, and sulfur dioxide, NOx and PM2.5 fell while GDP rose. [verify]
4. **What it missed**: agricultural ammonia is largely unregulated, ozone fell less than other pollutants, and wildfire smoke now offsets some gains. [verify]
5. **What this means for you**: as power and traffic emissions fall, farm ammonia and smoke make up a larger share of the remaining PM2.5, so agriculture is likely to draw more attention from air regulators.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- US emissions of the six common pollutants fell by roughly 70% between 1970 and 2020 while GDP grew several-fold. [verify]
- The Acid Rain Program's sulfur dioxide cap cut power plant emissions below the cap at lower cost than projected. [verify]
- Agricultural ammonia emissions in the US have not fallen in step with other PM2.5 precursors. [verify]
- EPA's own analyses estimate benefits of the 1990 amendments far above their costs. [verify]
