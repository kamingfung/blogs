**Promise:** Read an exposure–response curve as a relative risk against concentration, explain where its shape comes from, and say why curve choice drives health estimates.

**Trap:** Risk rises in a straight line from a safe threshold; the evidence shows no clear safe level and often a steeper rise per microgram at low concentrations.

**Assumes:** air-land-climate/units-standards-and-what-unhealthy-means.

## Outline

1. **What the curve says**: it gives the relative risk of death or disease at a given long-term concentration compared with a lower reference, usually from large cohort studies.
2. **Where the evidence comes from**: cohorts in clean countries cover the low end, while high concentrations rely on fewer studies, indoor smoke and active smoking, so the curve's upper part is stitched together. [verify]
3. **Shapes in use**: log-linear, supralinear and integrated curves give similar answers in the middle and very different answers at the ends.
4. **Confounding and composition**: income, smoking and location correlate with exposure, and the curve treats all PM2.5 mass as equally toxic, an assumption under debate.
5. **What this means for you**: the curve shape decides how much a small cut in clean air is worth compared with a large cut in dirty air; for a modeler, it is a structural assumption, not a fitted detail.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- Large cohort studies have not found a PM2.5 concentration below which mortality risk stops falling. [verify]
- Many current curves are supralinear, so each microgram removed at low concentrations buys more risk reduction than one removed at high concentrations. [verify]
- Curves for high concentrations borrow evidence from household air pollution and smoking studies. [verify]
- Standard curves treat PM2.5 of any composition as equally harmful per unit mass.
