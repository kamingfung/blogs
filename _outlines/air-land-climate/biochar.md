**Promise:** Explain how heating biomass without oxygen stores carbon, how much of that carbon counts as removed, and what biochar does to soil nitrogen and pH.

**Trap:** All the carbon in biochar is locked away for good and it improves every soil; only a stable fraction persists for centuries, and its liming effect can harm acid-loving crops.

**Assumes:** air-land-climate/fixed-nitrogen.

## Outline

1. **From plant to char**: pyrolysis heats biomass without oxygen and leaves a carbon-rich solid; the temperature and the feedstock set how stable that carbon is.
2. **How much is removed, and for how long**: a fraction of biochar carbon decays within years and the rest over centuries, so crediting depends on an assumed persistence and on what the biomass would otherwise have done. [verify]
3. **Effects on soil nitrogen**: biochar can lower nitrous oxide emissions and nitrate leaching but can raise ammonia loss where it lifts soil pH. [verify]
4. **Side effects and limits**: biochar is alkaline, darkens the surface, and competes with other uses of the same residues; supply of clean feedstock limits scale.
5. **What this means for you**: for blueberries, which need acid soil, an alkaline amendment works against the crop unless pH is managed; check pH and feedstock before applying it.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- Most biochar carbon from high-temperature pyrolysis persists for more than a century in soil. [verify]
- Meta-analyses report average reductions in soil nitrous oxide emissions after biochar addition. [verify]
- Biochar usually raises soil pH, which can increase ammonia volatilization.
- The net carbon removed depends on what would have happened to the feedstock without pyrolysis.
