**Promise:** Explain the main feedback loops between land and atmosphere, how modelers measure their strength, and why an offline land run can give a different answer from a coupled one.

**Trap:** The land is a passive floor under the weather; dry soil heats and dries the air above it, which can change rain and further dry the soil.

**Assumes:** air-land-climate/what-an-earth-system-model-is and air-land-climate/the-land-model-clm.

## Outline

1. **Offline versus coupled**: an offline land model reads fixed weather; a coupled one changes the weather it then receives, which closes feedback loops.
2. **Soil moisture and temperature**: when soil dries, less energy goes to evaporation and more to heating the air, so dry spells can amplify heat waves.
3. **Soil moisture and rain**: wetter soil can favor or suppress local rain depending on the boundary layer; the sign differs by region.
4. **Measuring coupling strength**: model experiments that fix or free soil moisture find hotspots in transition zones between wet and dry climates. [verify]
5. **What this means for you**: soil moisture changes slowly, which gives weeks-ahead weather and yield forecasts some memory; a forecaster can use soil moisture anomalies as a predictor, mostly in transition zones and in summer.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- In soil-moisture-limited regions, dry soils raise sensible heating and can amplify heat extremes. [verify]
- Land–atmosphere coupling is strongest in transition zones between wet and dry climates. [verify]
- A land experiment run offline cannot show feedbacks through weather, so its result can differ from the coupled result in sign as well as size.
- Soil moisture memory adds forecast skill at subseasonal lead times in strongly coupled regions. [verify]
