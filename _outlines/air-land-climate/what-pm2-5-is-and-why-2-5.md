**Promise:** Explain what a PM2.5 number measures, why the cut sits at 2.5 micrometers, and what the number leaves out.

**Trap:** PM2.5 sounds like one substance made of 2.5-micrometer particles; it is the total mass of everything that passes a size cut, whatever it is made of.

**Assumes:** School chemistry and the idea of a concentration. No series modules needed.

## Outline

1. **A sieve, not a substance**: PM2.5 is defined by how an inlet sorts particles by their aerodynamic size; the 2.5 is the size at which half get through.
2. **Particles come in modes**: airborne particle sizes cluster in a fine mode and a coarse mode with a dip between them, and the cut was placed near that dip so it separates combustion-and-chemistry particles from dust and sea spray.
3. **Where particles land in the body**: smaller particles reach deeper into the lungs, which is the health reason a fine-particle cut was added after the older PM10 standard.
4. **Mass hides number and makeup**: a few large particles carry most of the mass, while ultrafine particles carry most of the count; two air samples with the same PM2.5 can differ in chemistry and toxicity.
5. **What this means for you**: read a PM2.5 value as total fine mass over an averaging time, and ask what made it before comparing two places or two days; for a sensor in a field, humidity and dust can push the number away from what a reference inlet would report.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- PM2.5 is the mass concentration of particles whose aerodynamic diameter falls below about 2.5 micrometers, with 50% collection efficiency at the cut.
- The 2.5-micrometer cut sits near the natural minimum between the fine and coarse particle modes, so it mostly separates particles formed by combustion and chemistry from mechanically generated ones. [verify]
- The US added a PM2.5 standard alongside PM10 in 1997, after studies linked fine particles more closely to mortality. [verify]
- Equal PM2.5 mass does not imply equal particle number, composition or harm.
