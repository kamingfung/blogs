**Promise:** Compare a measured temperature profile with the dry and moist adiabats and say whether a nudged parcel will sink back, keep rising, or rise only once it is saturated.

**Trap:** "The lapse rate" is how fast rising air cools; in fact there are two different rates, the parcel's (set by physics) and the surrounding air's (measured, and different every day), and stability is the comparison between them.

**Assumes:** cloud-formation/why-does-rising-air-cool (dry and moist adiabatic lapse rates, lifting condensation level).

## Outline

1. **Two lapse rates, not one**: the parcel follows an adiabat; the environment is whatever a weather balloon measures; why mixing the two up causes most confusion.
2. **Buoyancy decides**: a parcel warmer than its surroundings is lighter and keeps rising; colder, it sinks back; stable, neutral and unstable as three outcomes of one test.
3. **Conditional instability**: a profile between the dry and moist adiabats is stable for dry air and unstable for saturated air, which is why cumulus can sit quietly all morning and then tower in the afternoon.
4. **Reading a sounding**: the level where a lifted parcel becomes warmer than its surroundings, the level where it stops, and the area between the two curves as the energy available to the storm, described in words before naming it.
5. **What this means for you**: afternoon showers, hail and gusts follow from daytime heating making the lower profile unstable; a forecast that mentions instability or convective energy is making exactly this comparison.
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- Stability is a comparison: a layer is stable when the environment cools with height more slowly than a rising parcel would, and unstable when it cools faster.
- Air that is stable when dry can be unstable once saturated, because the moist adiabat is shallower than the dry adiabat.
- Surface heating during the day steepens the lower profile, so instability and convective showers peak in the afternoon over land [verify].
- The area between the parcel and environment curves on a sounding measures the energy a rising parcel can gain, and larger values allow stronger updrafts.
