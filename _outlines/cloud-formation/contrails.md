**Promise:** Explain why some aircraft leave a trail that vanishes in seconds while others leave one that spreads into a sheet of cirrus for hours, and why that difference matters for climate.

**Trap:** A contrail is exhaust smoke; it is a cloud formed when hot, moist exhaust mixes with very cold air, like breath on a winter day, and whether it lasts depends on the air, not the engine.

**Assumes:** cloud-formation/how-much-water-can-air-hold (saturation and its steep dependence on temperature).

## Outline

1. **A mixing cloud**: hot, humid exhaust and cold, dry air each sit below saturation, yet their mixture can exceed it, because the saturation curve bends; the contrail forms behind the engine within a fraction of a second [verify].
2. **When a contrail can form**: a temperature threshold set by altitude, humidity and engine efficiency; why contrails appear at cruise altitude but not near the ground [verify].
3. **Short-lived or persistent**: in dry air the ice crystals evaporate quickly; in air that is saturated with respect to ice they persist and spread into contrail cirrus.
4. **Warming or cooling**: contrails reflect sunlight by day and trap outgoing heat day and night; the night-time and winter effects and the overall net warming estimate [verify].
5. **What this means for you**: no direct farm tie; for forecasting and climate work, contrail cirrus is a non-CO2 aviation effect, and routing flights around ice-saturated air is an active research topic [verify].
6. **Key takeaways**

## Key takeaways

- A contrail forms when exhaust mixes with ambient air and the mixture passes saturation, even though neither air mass was saturated on its own.
- Contrails persist and spread only where the ambient air is saturated with respect to ice; in drier air they vanish within seconds to minutes.
- Persistent contrails and the cirrus they spread into have a net warming effect on climate, estimated to be of a similar order to the warming from aviation's CO2 [verify].
- A small fraction of flights produces most of the contrail warming, because ice-saturated regions are thin and patchy [verify].
